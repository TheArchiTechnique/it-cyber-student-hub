Networks

A network connects devices so they can exchange data and share services. The name may describe its coverage, its purpose, or how devices connect.

TypeWhat it describesTypical example
LANA local area network within a limited area.Office computers connected to a switch.
WLANA wireless local area network using Wi-Fi.Laptops connected through an access point.
WANA wide area network that connects networks across a large area.A provider connection between distant offices.
PANA personal area network around one user.A phone and a Bluetooth headset.
MANA metropolitan area network covering a city or metro area.A city network linking municipal buildings.
SANA storage area network that gives servers access to block storage.Servers connected to shared storage arrays.

Wi-Fi is a connection method. A WLAN can be part of the same local network as wired computers. A network does not become a WAN simply because one connection uses fiber.

Network hardware

DeviceJob
SwitchConnects wired devices on a LAN and forwards Ethernet frames using MAC addresses. A managed switch adds controls such as VLANs.
RouterForwards packets between IP networks. A small-office router commonly provides the default gateway, NAT, DHCP, and firewall features.
Access pointConnects Wi-Fi clients to the wired network. A standalone access point is not automatically an internet router or modem.
FirewallAllows or blocks traffic according to rules. It can be a separate appliance or a function built into another device.
Cable modemConnects a cable internet service to Ethernet. The provider side uses coaxial cable.
DSL modemConnects a DSL service on a telephone line to the local network.
ONTAn optical network terminal connects the customer to a provider’s passive optical fiber network. It commonly supplies an Ethernet handoff.
Patch panelOrganizes permanent cable terminations. Patch cords connect its ports to the switch. A passive panel does not route or switch traffic.
NICA network interface connects a device to the network. It may be Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or another supported interface.
PoE switch / injectorSupplies power over compatible Ethernet copper cabling. A PoE switch combines data and power; an injector adds power to a link.

Power over Ethernet

Standard PoE (802.3af) supplies up to 15.4 W at the switch port, with up to 12.95 W available to the device after cable losses. PoE+ (802.3at) increases those limits to 30 W at the port and 25.5 W at the device. Check the power each device needs as well as the total switch power budget.

Choosing equipment

Count all required ports, including uplinks. Match interface speeds and media. For PoE, check both the power available per port and the switch’s total power budget. An access point also needs the right wireless bands, client capacity, and placement.

Installation details

For standard 100 m (about 328 ft) twisted-pair Ethernet cabling, count the entire cable path between devices. This commonly includes up to 90 m (about 295 ft) of fixed cabling plus 10 m (about 33 ft) of patch cords. Check the actual port specifications too; some copper transceiver modules have shorter limits.

UTP uses twisted pairs without an added shield. Shielded cabling adds protection against interference and needs the correct installation and bonding. Fiber resists electromagnetic interference but needs compatible optics and clean connectors. It does not carry PoE power.

Use the jacket rating required for the route. Plenum-rated cable is for spaces that require that fire and smoke rating. Outdoor and direct-burial routes need cable approved for those conditions.

Wireless configuration

BandCommon useTradeoff
2.4 GHzOlder clients and devices that need broader coverage.Usually travels through obstacles better, but has fewer channel choices and shares spectrum with devices such as Bluetooth.
5 GHzModern office laptops and higher-capacity Wi-Fi.More channel choices than 2.4 GHz. Coverage depends on walls, power, and placement.
6 GHzCompatible Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 clients.Additional spectrum without older 2.4/5 GHz-only clients. Both the client and access point need 6 GHz support. More available spectrum can help with large transfers in a busy area; the band alone does not guarantee the fastest speed.

802.11b/g use 2.4 GHz. 802.11a/ac use 5 GHz. 802.11n can use 2.4 or 5 GHz. Wi-Fi 6 uses 802.11ax on 2.4/5 GHz; Wi-Fi 6E extends it to 6 GHz. Wi-Fi 7 uses 802.11be and can operate in all three bands, depending on the device.

Channels and placement

In the United States, 1, 6, and 11 are the usual non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channel choices at 20 MHz. Wider channels can improve throughput, but consume more spectrum and leave fewer separate channels for nearby access points.

Use a site survey or Wi-Fi analyzer to check coverage and interference. Place access points near the users they serve, away from major obstructions. More transmit power is not always better: clients must be able to send data back to the access point.

T568A and T568B

These standards define which colored conductor connects to each of the eight pins. They have the same performance. The green and orange pairs change positions; the blue and brown pairs stay in place.

Use the numbered pins as your reference. For the plug view used in this activity, the gold contact side faces up, the latch is underneath, and the cable enters from the bottom. Pin 1 is on the left.

Pairs matter

The four pairs occupy pins 1/2, 3/6, 4/5, and 7/8. Keep each pair twisted as close to the termination as the connector’s instructions require. Simply connecting pin 1 to pin 1 is not enough if the wrong conductors form the pairs. A split pair can cause interference and poor performance.

Straight-through and crossover

ArrangementEndsTypical use
Straight-throughA–A or B–BNormal Ethernet patch cables. On legacy 10/100 ports without Auto-MDI-X: computer to switch, or router to switch.
Two-pair crossoverA–BLegacy 10BASE-T / 100BASE-TX links with matching port roles: computer to computer or normal switch port to switch port. Pins 1/2 cross with 3/6.
RolloverPin order reversedSome console connections. It is not a substitute for an Ethernet patch cable.

Modern Auto-MDI-X ports adjust the pair assignments automatically. A normal straight-through cable is usually all you need, even between switches. The A–B example describes the traditional two-pair crossover for 10/100 Ethernet; do not treat it as a universal crossover wiring diagram for every Ethernet speed.

Check the finished cable

A wire-map tester checks the conductor connections and can reveal opens, shorts, reversals, or crossed pairs. A tester that supports split-pair detection can identify that fault too. Use a cable certifier when you need to verify that an installed link meets a category’s performance requirements.
