Investigate before you act

Classify first, then select evidence for suspected phishing. After submission, use the explanations to connect the visible indicators with attack types and persuasion tactics. Terminology is taught in feedback, not graded as another selection.

1 · Phishing, spam, and legitimate mail

Phishing uses deceptive communications to obtain account secrets, deliver malware, or induce unsafe actions such as unauthorized payments. Spam is unwanted bulk messaging, commonly advertising. Ordinary marketing does not become phishing simply because it is unsolicited.

Legitimate email fits a reasonable business purpose, consistent addresses and destinations, and an appropriate request. Classify using the available evidence, while recognizing that absence of obvious red flags cannot guarantee authenticity.

External senders, deadlines, attachments, and different reply addresses can all occur in legitimate mail. Assess combinations of indicators. Do not open a destination or run a file to test whether it is safe.

2 · Attacks and persuasion

Social engineering manipulates a person into disclosing information or taking an unsafe action. The attack category describes what is happening; the persuasion tactic describes how the person is influenced; the technical indicator is an observable clue.

Pretexting invents a story or business situation to justify a request. Impersonation means pretending to be a trusted identity. Authority exploits an apparent position of responsibility. Urgency creates time pressure; a deadline alone does not prove phishing.

Several techniques can occur together. These supporting tactics help explain the scenarios; pretexting, urgency, and typosquatting are not separately enumerated items in the official 220-1202 Version 2.0 objective list.

3 · Phishing variants and account secrets

Spear phishing tailors a lure to a person or organization. Whaling targets executives or other high-value individuals. A message merely claiming to come from an executive is not automatically whaling.

Business Email Compromise (BEC) uses an impersonated or compromised business identity to obtain money or sensitive information. It can work without links, attachments, or malware. Credential harvesting collects passwords or other account-access information through deception.

Multifactor Authentication (MFA) combines different authentication factor types. A one-time code or approval is an access secret, not information to send to a help desk. An attacker may use a code immediately to establish a session.

Quick Response (QR) code phishing places a deceptive destination in a scannable image. Smishing uses Short Message Service (SMS), or text messages; vishing uses voice calls or messages.

4 · Sender identity and verification

A display name is not identity proof. Compare the complete sender and Reply-To addresses, then check trusted contact records independently. A different Reply-To can be a legitimate ticketing or billing workflow, but can also redirect replies to an attacker.

Typosquatting uses deceptively similar domain names, including character substitutions. For example, maplew0rks.example uses a zero in place of the o in mapleworks.example. A different domain is not proof that the From address was forged.

Spoofing falsifies identifying information to appear to be another source. Lookalike-domain impersonation and spoofing are different concepts. A familiar From address could also belong to a compromised account; the visible address alone cannot establish which mechanism occurred.

Email authentication checks can evaluate authorized sending servers, message signatures, and sender-domain alignment. Review full headers through approved tools. A pass does not establish honest intent or exclude a compromised account. An email security gateway filters mail and records delivery information that authorized staff can review.

5 · Hyperlinks and attachments

Displayed link text can differ from the actual destination. Inspect the full hostname, the site name after https:// and before the next /. In https://mapleworks.example.accounts-check.example/login, the host is under accounts-check.example. The familiar words at the left do not give Mapleworks control of that domain.

HTTPS, Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure, encrypts the connection; it does not prove the operator is trustworthy. In this activity, every destination has an expandable inspection control that works with a mouse, keyboard, or touch. The QR illustration cannot be scanned.

Inspect the entire filename. Minutes.pdf.exe ends in an executable extension; .js and .ps1 are scripts. Unexpected execution requests or instructions to bypass security protection are strong concerns. A PDF extension alone does not establish safety, and a script extension does not identify a particular malware family.

6 · Response, containment, and documentation

Use the organization’s reporting process to preserve suspicious mail and document who received it, when it arrived, and whether anyone interacted. Verify unusual requests using a known directory number, bookmarked portal, vendor record, or existing ticket.

If credentials were disclosed, promptly escalate and coordinate account remediation under the approved procedure: password reset, session revocation, and review of authentication factors and sign-ins may be required. A password reset alone may not terminate an attacker’s session.

If suspicious code ran, follow authorized containment steps to limit exposure and escalate. Do not independently wipe or power off a system to investigate it. Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) tools help authorized responders monitor and isolate endpoints. Antimalware protection, email gateways, user education, and antiphishing training reduce exposure.

Use junk/block controls for unwanted bulk spam. Avoid untrusted unsubscribe links. A Tier 1 technician follows established procedures and escalates changes beyond their authority.

Foundation: Security 2.4 (malware detection, removal, and prevention) and 2.5 (social engineering, threats, and vulnerabilities) in the official CompTIA A+ Core 2 (220-1202) objectives. Response practice also applies relevant incident-handling and documentation principles.
