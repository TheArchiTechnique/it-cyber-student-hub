Assessment

Investigate before you act

Compare the request, technical details, and a known business workflow. No single cosmetic feature proves that a message is safe.

1 · Social engineering and phishing

Social engineering manipulates people into disclosing information or taking an unsafe action. Phishing uses deceptive messages to steal credentials or sensitive data, deliver malware, or obtain unauthorized payments. Professional writing and familiar branding do not establish authenticity.

Authority, urgency, fear, secrecy, or a reward can pressure a person into skipping normal checks. Legitimate notices also have deadlines. Interpret these cues alongside the destination, requested action, and independent records.

2 · Sender, domain, and Reply-To

A display name can say anything. Inspect the full sender address and compare its domain with a trusted directory. A familiar From address alone does not prove sender authentication or exclude a compromised account.

Lookalike domains substitute characters or add words. A Reply-To mismatch can redirect replies to an attacker, but approved ticketing and marketing services also use different addresses. Check the documented provider relationship before deciding.

For example, help@mapleworks.example and help@maplew0rks.example belong to different domains. A logo cannot resolve that difference.

3 · Link and attachment inspection

Displayed link text may differ from its actual destination. Inspect without opening. On a computer, a preview may appear on hover; touch and keyboard users need an accessible inspection control. This activity provides expandable destination details for every simulated link and QR image.

In https://mapleworks.example.accounts-check.example/login, the host is under accounts-check.example. The company name at the left does not make it a company site. HTTPS encrypts a connection; it does not establish honest intent.

Inspect the full attachment filename. Minutes.pdf.exe is an executable, not a PDF; .js and .ps1 are script extensions. Unexpected scripts, executables, or requests to enable macros or bypass protection are strong concerns. A PDF extension alone does not guarantee safety.

4 · Phishing variations

Spear phishing tailors a lure to a person or organization. Whaling targets executives or other high-value people; a message merely claiming to be from an executive is not automatically whaling.

Business email compromise (BEC) uses an impersonated or compromised business identity to request money or sensitive information. It may contain no link, attachment, or malware. Changed bank details and attempts to bypass approvals require independent verification.

QR code phishing hides the destination in a scannable image and may move the victim to a personal phone. Inspect the destination and verify the workflow. Smishing uses SMS/text messages; vishing uses voice calls or voice messages.

5 · Spam, phishing, and legitimate mail

Spam is unwanted bulk messaging, often advertising. It does not necessarily involve credential theft or another fraudulent objective. For this investigation, use Phishing when deception seeks an unsafe action; use Spam for verified unsolicited bulk advertising without those characteristics.

A promotional catalog verified as ordinary advertising belongs in junk. A fake survey collecting company passwords is phishing. An expected supplier invoice matching an independently confirmed order may be legitimate even though the sender is external.

The absence of an obvious red flag is not universal proof of legitimacy. Use the independent case context supplied with each message. Select suspicious evidence only when that detail supports an attack; ordinary advertising can have no suspicious technical indicators.

6 · Respond and build awareness

Follow the organization’s reporting procedure for suspected phishing. Preserve the message for the security team using the approved reporting mechanism. Do not reply, forward it casually, open its attachment, or visit its destination to investigate it.

Verify unusual requests through a known phone number, bookmarked portal, or existing ticket. Do not use contact information supplied by the suspicious message. Never share passwords or MFA codes. If someone already interacted, promptly report what happened through the incident process.

Use junk/block controls for unwanted bulk spam. Do not rely on unsubscribe links in untrusted messages. Keep verified legitimate mail available. Email security gateways and endpoint protection reduce exposure, while employee security awareness and antiphishing training help people recognize and report attempts.

Scope: CompTIA A+ Core 2 (220-1202), Security 2.4 (protective methods and antiphishing training) and 2.5 (social engineering and BEC). Official exam objectives.
